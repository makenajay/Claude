How to use this chapter

This chapter treats ANOVA not as "a test for three or more means" but as an accounting system for variation. Every idea (the F test, regression, interactions, the intraclass correlation and the survey design effect) comes from the same single identity: total variation = variation we can attribute to structure + variation we cannot.

1Why ANOVA exists

Suppose you observe different outcomes across people, farms, schools, machines, constituencies, hospitals, or survey clusters. The interesting question is never simply whether observations differ (they always do). The useful question is:

How much of the observed variation lines up with some systematic structure, and how much is left over?

Analysis of variance (ANOVA) answers by splitting a total sum of squared deviations into pieces. In the simplest case it compares how far apart the group means are with how scattered the observations inside each group are. If the means are far apart relative to the scatter inside groups, the grouping variable carries information about the outcome.

The same algebra underlies ordinary least-squares regression: a categorical factor is just a set of 0/1 indicator variables. Multiple regression, factorial ANOVA, ANCOVA, mixed models and multilevel survey models are all extensions of this one principle, and Part III shows that the survey design effect comes from it too.

2Variance from first principles

2.1Why deviations must be squared

For observations $y_1,\dots,y_n$ with mean $\bar y$, the raw deviations $y_i-\bar y$ always add to exactly zero: the positives cancel the negatives. Squaring them stops the cancelling and gives extra weight to large departures. The resulting total is the total sum of squares:

$$\SST=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_i-\bar y)^2,\qquad s^2=\frac{\SST}{n-1}.$$

You can take "squared deviation" literally: each one is the area of a square whose side is the distance from the mean.

Bottom: each deviation turned into a square whose side is that distance, so its area is the squared deviation. Points far from the mean (15) contribute far more than points near it (8).')]]

2.3Why divide by n − 1?

Once $\bar y$ has been computed from the data, only $n-1$ of the deviations are free to vary: the last one is forced so that they all sum to zero. These free pieces are the degrees of freedom. Dividing by $n-1$ rather than $n$ makes $s^2$ an unbiased estimate of the population variance $\sigma^2$. The same bookkeeping, "sum of squares ÷ its degrees of freedom", produces every mean square in ANOVA.

3The ANOVA decomposition

Let $y_{ij}$ be observation $j$ in group $i$ ($i=1,\dots,k$; group $i$ has $n_i$ units; $n=\sum n_i$). Write $\bar y_i$ for the group mean and $\bar y$ for the grand mean. Add and subtract the group mean:

$$\underbrace{y_{ij}-\bar y}_{\color{#4a3aa7}\text{total}}\;=\;\underbrace{(\bar y_i-\bar y)}_{\color{#b8461a}\text{between}}\;+\;\underbrace{(y_{ij}-\bar y_i)}_{\color{#1c5cab}\text{within}}$$
[[fig('f_decomp','Figure 2. The decomposition, for one farm. The highlighted plot under Fertilizer B yielded 26 q/acre. It sits +4.33 above the grand mean (violet). That total splits into +2.93 because fertilizer B does better than average (orange, between) and +1.40 because this plot beat its own group mean (blue, within). Every one of the 15 points can be split the same way.')]]

Square both sides and sum over every observation. The cross-product term disappears, leaving the fundamental identity:

$$\SST \;=\; \SSB \;+\; \SSW,\qquad \SSB=\sum_{i} n_i(\bar y_i-\bar y)^2,\quad \SSW=\sum_i\sum_j (y_{ij}-\bar y_i)^2.$$
[[call('proof','Why the cross-product vanishes (the step most books skip)','proof')]]

Expanding the square gives $\SST=\SSB+\SSW+2\sum_i\sum_j(\bar y_i-\bar y)(y_{ij}-\bar y_i)$. For a fixed group $i$, the factor $(\bar y_i-\bar y)$ is a constant, so it comes out of the inner sum: $2\sum_i(\bar y_i-\bar y)\,\big[\sum_j (y_{ij}-\bar y_i)\big]$. The bracket is the sum of deviations of group $i$ from its own mean, which is always zero (Section 2.1). So the whole cross term is zero, for any data. ∎

4The F statistic and the hypothesis test

The one-way null hypothesis is $H_0:\mu_1=\mu_2=\dots=\mu_k$; the alternative is that at least one population mean differs. The test statistic is a ratio of mean squares:

$$F=\frac{\MSB}{\MSW}=\frac{\SSB/(k-1)}{\SSW/(n-k)}\;\sim\;F_{k-1,\;n-k}\ \text{ under } H_0.$$

Why should F be near 1 when H0 is true? Look at what each mean square estimates on average:

$$E[\MSW]=\sigma^2,\qquad E[\MSB]=\sigma^2+\frac{\sum_i n_i(\mu_i-\bar\mu)^2}{k-1}.$$

When all $\mu_i$ are equal the extra term is zero, so numerator and denominator estimate the same $\sigma^2$ and their ratio hovers around 1. Real differences between means inflate only the numerator, pushing F upward. F is therefore literally (noise + signal) ÷ noise.

[[fig('f_fdist','Figure 3. The reference distribution. If all three fertilizers were equally good, F would follow the F(2, 12) curve: usually near 1, and above [[O["fert"]["Fcrit"]|.2f]] only 5% of the time (orange). The observed F = [[O["fert"]["F"]|.1f]] is far beyond that, so data like these would be very surprising under H₀.')]]

The p-value is the probability, assuming $H_0$ and the model's assumptions, of an F at least as large as the one observed. For the fertilizer data $p=[[O['fert']['p']|.5f]]$.

[[call('warn','Common mistakes with p-values','alert')]]
  • A small p says the means are not all equal. It does not say which differ (Section 6.3) or by how much (Section 6.2).
  • A large p is not proof that the means are equal; it may just mean the study was too small or too noisy (Example 11).
  • p is not the probability that H0 is true.

5What is signal and what is noise?

This is the conceptual heart of ANOVA. In a statistical model, signal means variation represented by the factors or predictors you specified. Noise means the residual variation left after fitting them. But "residual" does not automatically mean "meaningless randomness". It can contain measurement error, genuine individual differences, omitted predictors, curvature, interactions, time trends, and pure chance.

[[fig('f_signalnoise','Figure 4. F compares the gaps between means (signal) with the scatter inside groups (noise). All four panels use the same shapes of scatter. Moving right triples the within-group spread; moving down shrinks the gaps between means. The top-right panel has the same means as the top-left, yet F collapses ninefold because the noise has tripled. And the top-right and bottom-left panels have identical F: what matters is the ratio of gap to spread, not either one alone.')]]

5.1How ANOVA estimates noise

Units that receive the same treatment give a natural estimate of background variability: their differences from their own group mean cannot be explained by the group factor. So SSW and MSW estimate the error variance, provided the model is correct and within-group observations are independent with similar variance.

5.2We cannot label an individual residual as "noise"

No calculation can look at one residual and certify that it is noise. The separation is statistical and depends on the model. Confidence that the residual variance really is noise comes from good design and checking:

Replication

Repeated comparable units measure background scatter.

Randomisation

Breaks links between treatment and hidden causes.

Blocking

Moves predictable variation (field, day, district) out of the residual.

Multilevel models

Separate person, household, village and PSU variance.

Residual plots

Reveal trends, funnels, outliers or curvature left behind.

Validation

Does the signal predict new data, or just this sample?

Reliability data

Repeat measurements show how much is instrument noise.

Intervals & power

Show how precisely the signal is pinned down.
