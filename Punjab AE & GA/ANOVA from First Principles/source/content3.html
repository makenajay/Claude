9Clustering, ICC and the design effect

Surveys rarely sample people independently. They sample PSUs (primary sampling units: villages, polling booths, census blocks, schools, households) and then interview several people inside each. People from the same PSU tend to resemble each other: they share a neighbourhood, a local candidate, the same news and the same caste mix. That resemblance means the observations are not independent in the way a simple random sample (SRS) of the same size would be. ANOVA is exactly the tool that measures how much they resemble each other.

9.1The random-intercept model

$$y_{ij}=\mu+\textcolor{#b8461a}{u_j}+\textcolor{#1c5cab}{e_{ij}},\qquad u_j\sim(0,\sigma_b^2),\quad e_{ij}\sim(0,\sigma_w^2)\quad\Rightarrow\quad \operatorname{Var}(y_{ij})=\textcolor{#b8461a}{\sigma_b^2}+\textcolor{#1c5cab}{\sigma_w^2}$$

Here $u_j$ is cluster $j$'s shared deviation (its "local flavour") and $e_{ij}$ is person $i$'s individual deviation. The intraclass correlation is the share of total variance that sits between clusters:

$$\rho=\frac{\sigma_b^2}{\sigma_b^2+\sigma_w^2}\qquad(\text{also the correlation between two people in the same cluster})$$
[[fig('f_iccbars','Figure 11. ICC as a share of variance. For many attitude items the between-cluster share is small (ρ ≈ 0.01–0.05). Vote intention by polling booth is often higher (ρ ≈ 0.05–0.15), because booths are socially homogeneous. The toy example in §9.4 is deliberately extreme.')]]

9.2Estimating the ICC from a one-way random-effects ANOVA

Treat PSU as the "group" in a one-way ANOVA. With equal cluster size $m$, $E[\MSW]=\sigma_w^2$ and $E[\MSB]=\sigma_w^2+m\sigma_b^2$. Solving these gives the estimates:

$$\hat\sigma_w^2=\MSW,\qquad \hat\sigma_b^2=\max\!\Big(0,\ \frac{\MSB-\MSW}{m}\Big),\qquad \hat\rho=\frac{\MSB-\MSW}{\MSB+(m-1)\MSW}.$$

The truncation at zero matters: when MSB < MSW by chance, the raw estimate of σ²b would be negative, which is impossible for a variance.

9.3From ICC to the design effect: a short derivation

The mean of the $m$ people in one cluster shares the single $u_j$, so its variance is $\sigma_b^2+\sigma_w^2/m$. Rewrite that using $\sigma^2=\sigma_b^2+\sigma_w^2$ and $\rho$:

$$\operatorname{Var}(\bar y_j)=\sigma_b^2+\frac{\sigma_w^2}{m}=\frac{\sigma^2}{m}\big[1+(m-1)\rho\big]\ \ \Rightarrow\ \ \operatorname{Var}(\bar y)=\underbrace{\frac{\sigma^2}{n}}_{\text{SRS}}\times\underbrace{\big[1+(m-1)\rho\big]}_{\textstyle \text{DEFF}}$$

for $K$ independent clusters and $n=Km$. So the design effect is the factor by which clustering inflates the variance of the mean. The effective sample size is the SRS size that would give the same precision:

$$\text{DEFF}=1+(m-1)\rho,\qquad \text{DEFT}=\sqrt{\text{DEFF}},\qquad n_{\text{eff}}=\frac{n}{\text{DEFF}}.$$

The formula is exact under this model (equal cluster sizes, a common ρ, clusters sampled independently). In real surveys it is an approximation. DEFT is what multiplies standard errors and confidence-interval widths.

[[fig('f_deff','Figure 12. DEFF rises with ρ and with the number of interviews per cluster. Even a modest ρ = 0.10 doubles the variance when m = 11, and nearly triples it at m = 20. Small ICCs are not harmless when clusters are large.')]]

9.6"Real" differences can still hurt precision

Between-PSU differences are genuine population heterogeneity, not measurement noise. Yet they create positive covariance among respondents who share a PSU, which inflates the sampling variance of an overall mean. DEFF does not say the between-cluster component is fake. It says each extra interview in the same PSU adds less new information, because part of it repeats what that PSU has already told you.

9.7Unequal cluster sizes, weights and stratification

Unequal cluster sizes

Information concentrates in a few large clusters, raising DEFF. Kish's approximation, with CVm the coefficient of variation of cluster sizes: $1+\{(1+CV_m^2)\bar m-1\}\rho$

Unequal weights

Varying weights add variance. Kish's weighting effect is $1+CV_w^2$; a rough overall DEFF is often taken as the product of the weighting and clustering parts.

Stratification

Usually reduces variance, when strata (region, urban/rural) are internally similar and differ from each other.

For a real survey, estimate the DEFF of each statistic directly as $\operatorname{Var}_{\text{design}}(\hat\theta)/\operatorname{Var}_{\text{SRS}}(\hat\theta)$, using software that knows the strata, PSUs and weights (Taylor linearisation or replicate weights; the R survey package does this). The DEFF is statistic-specific: vote share, age and turnout each have their own ρ.

The SRS formula $1.96\sqrt{p(1-p)/n}$ gives ± 1.9 points here, but the honest design-based margin is closer to ± 2.7. The difference matters when two parties are separated by 3–4 points. Always multiply by DEFT, or better, let survey software compute the standard error from the actual design.

10.2How many interviews per booth? The cost–precision trade-off

Opening a booth (travel, permissions, supervisor time) costs $c_1$; each extra interview there costs $c_2$. For a fixed budget, the variance of the mean is minimised by the classic result:

$$m_{\text{opt}}=\sqrt{\frac{c_1}{c_2}\cdot\frac{1-\rho}{\rho}}=\sqrt{\frac{6000}{300}\cdot\frac{0.94}{0.06}}\approx\mathbf{[[cost['mopt']|.0f]]}\ \text{interviews per booth}.$$
[[fig('f_cost','Figure 15. For a fixed budget, variance is U-shaped in m. Too few interviews per booth wastes money on travel; too many wastes interviews on people who resemble each other. The higher the ICC, the smaller the best m. Note how flat each curve is near its minimum: for ρ = 0.06, any m from about 11 to 25 is within 6% of the best possible variance.')]]

When ρ > 0, more PSUs with fewer respondents each is usually more efficient than a few PSUs sampled deeply, up to the point where field costs bite. Near the optimum the gains are small (here only a few percent over m = 12). Far from it, for example 50 interviews in each of 91 booths, the loss is large.
